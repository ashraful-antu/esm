Chapter 3: Temperature & Work Environment Design

IPE 407 — Ergonomics & Safety Management  |  ZAR (Rafi Madam)  |  Exam Importance: ★★★★

Overview

Human productivity and safety are significantly affected by the thermal environment. This chapter covers the physiological basis of heat and cold stress, the specific illnesses they cause, and the ergonomic principles for designing a thermally comfortable workplace.

Thermal Balance Equation

The human body maintains a core temperature of ~37°C. The thermal balance is governed by:

S = M − E ± R ± C − W
SymbolMeaningNotes
SHeat Storage (body temperature change)S > 0: body heats up; S < 0: body cools down; S = 0: equilibrium
MMetabolic heat productionAlways positive; ranges from ~80W (sleeping) to ~1200W (heavy exercise)
EEvaporative heat loss (sweating)Always a loss; dominant in hot environments
RRadiant heat exchange+ = gain (hot surroundings); − = loss (cold surroundings)
CConvective heat exchange+ = gain (hot air); − = loss (cool air)
WMechanical work doneWork removes heat from body (subtracted); usually small
Goal of thermal work environment design: Ensure S ≈ 0 (thermal equilibrium) during working hours, maintaining core temperature within ±1°C of 37°C.

Heat Stress

Heat stress occurs when the body's thermoregulatory capacity is overwhelmed. The body responds to heat through:

  • Vasodilation: Blood vessels in skin dilate to increase heat dissipation
  • Sweating: Evaporative cooling — primary defense in hot environments
  • Increased cardiac output: Heart works harder to pump blood to both skin (cooling) and muscles (work)
  • Reduced blood viscosity

When these mechanisms fail to maintain thermal equilibrium, heat illnesses develop.

Heat Illnesses ★★★★ Exam (3×)

Most Examined in Ch3 (3×): Explain different health issues/symptoms and first aid procedures for different heat illnesses.
🔥 Heat Stroke
Core temp > 41°C (106°F). Thermoregulation fails completely. Skin: hot, red, DRY (sweating stops). Confusion, seizures, unconsciousness. LIFE-THREATENING — medical emergency.
💧 Heat Exhaustion
Cardiovascular strain — heart can't meet demand of skin + muscle circulation. Cool, moist skin, rapid weak pulse, heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea. Core temp normal or slightly elevated.
😵 Heat Syncope
Fainting (syncope) due to peripheral vasodilation pooling blood in extremities, reducing venous return and brain blood flow. Occurs on sudden standing or prolonged standing in heat.
💨 Heat Hyperventilation
Rapid breathing in heat causes excess CO₂ exhalation → respiratory alkalosis. Symptoms: dizziness, tingling, muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm.
🌡️ Prickly Heat
Miliaria rubra. Skin rash from blocked sweat ducts after prolonged sweating. Red, itchy papules. Reduces sweating capacity → increases heat stroke risk. Common in tropical climates.
💊 Heat Cramps
Painful muscle cramps from electrolyte loss (sodium) through excessive sweating. Often occurs after heavy work. Treatment: oral electrolyte replacement.

Heat Stroke — Key Details

Definition of Heat Stroke (Heat Hyperpyrexia): A life-threatening condition where core body temperature exceeds 41°C (106°F) and the central thermoregulation mechanism fails.

Hallmark Sign: Skin is HOT, RED, and DRY (absence of sweating distinguishes heat stroke from heat exhaustion).

First Aid:
1. Move person to cool environment immediately
2. Remove excess clothing
3. Aggressive cooling: ice packs to neck, groin, armpits; cool water spray with fanning
4. Do NOT give fluids orally if unconscious
5. Emergency medical care — IV fluids, monitoring
6. Cool to 38.5°C then stop aggressive cooling (risk of overshoot hypothermia)

Heat Exhaustion — Key Details

Mechanism: Cardiovascular system is overloaded — blood must simultaneously supply working muscles AND skin for thermoregulation. Plasma volume decreases from sweating. Result: insufficient cardiac output for both demands.

Symptoms: Heavy sweating, weakness, cold/pale/clammy skin, fast/weak pulse, nausea, dizziness, possible fainting.

First Aid:
1. Move to cool area, rest
2. Loosen clothing, apply cool wet cloths
3. Oral rehydration with electrolyte drink
4. Lie down, elevate legs slightly
5. Medical attention if no improvement in 15 min

Heat Acclimatization ★★★ Exam (2×)

PYQ (2×): What is heat acclimatization? What is its significance on a production floor?

Heat acclimatization is the physiological adaptation process by which the body gradually adjusts to working in a hot environment. It involves a series of adaptations over approximately 2 weeks of repeated heat exposure.

Physiological Changes During Acclimatization

ParameterBefore AcclimatizationAfter Acclimatization
Sweat capacity~1 L/hr~2 L/hr (doubles)
Sweat onset temperatureHigher (delayed)Lower (earlier onset)
Salt content of sweatHigher (more NaCl lost)Lower (conserves electrolytes)
Heart rateHigher for same workloadLower (more efficient cardiovascular)
Core temperature riseGreater for same workloadSmaller (better thermoregulation)
Plasma volumeLowerHigher (better blood volume)

Significance on Production Floor

  • Reduces incidence of heat illnesses among workers
  • Improves work capacity and productivity in hot environments
  • Reduces physiological strain (heart rate, core temperature) for same workload
  • New workers should NOT be immediately assigned to full work in hot environments — requires a 2-week gradual acclimatization protocol
  • OSHA/NIOSH recommend starting at 20% of heat exposure time and increasing by ~20%/day for new workers
  • Salt supplementation needed only in unacclimatized workers — acclimatized workers conserve salt in sweat
Note: Heat acclimatization is a physiological process of adaptation, not merely psychological. The body physically changes its thermoregulatory capacity. (Exam True/False: "Heat acclimatization is a psychological process" — FALSE: it is physiological.)

Thermal Comfort Conditions

Thermal comfort is the condition in which a person is neither too hot nor too cold. ASHRAE defines it as "that condition of mind that expresses satisfaction with the thermal environment."

Comfortable Temperature

  • Sedentary work: 21–24°C (70–75°F)
  • Light work: ~18–21°C
  • Heavy work: Lower temperatures preferred

Humidity and Air Movement ★★★

Relative Humidity (RH)

RH LevelEffect
Optimal: 40–50%Comfortable; sweat evaporates effectively; skin does not dry out
< 20%Dryness of mucous membranes, skin cracking, static electricity
> 80%Fatigue; sweating becomes ineffective (air already saturated); body cannot cool itself efficiently
> 90%Heat illness risk dramatically increases even at moderate temperatures

Air Movement

  • < 0.1 m/s: Stuffiness (insufficient air movement)
  • 0.1–0.5 m/s: Generally acceptable; enhances evaporative cooling
  • > 1.5 m/s: Draft, discomfort (especially in cold environments)
At moderate temperatures, air movement enhances evaporative and convective heat loss, increasing the apparent comfort temperature by several degrees. This is the basis for the "wind chill factor" in cold and "heat index" in heat.

Cold Stress and Cold Injuries

Cold injuries are classified into two categories:

Freezing Injuries

Frostbite: Actual freezing of tissue. Ice crystals form in cells and extracellular spaces, causing mechanical cell damage and vascular injury. Full recovery may take months.

Frostnip: Milder form — skin surface blanches and loses sensation but tissue does not freeze deeply. Reversible within ~30 minutes of warming.

Non-Freezing Injuries

Cold Sensitization (Raynaud's phenomenon): Repeated cold exposure damages peripheral vasomotor regulation. Fingers become hypersensitive to cold even after warming; triggered by mild cold exposure years later. Vasospasm of digital arteries → white → blue → red sequence.

Trench Foot (Immersion Foot): Prolonged cold, wet conditions (above freezing). Tissue damage from vasoconstriction rather than freezing.

Frostbite — Stages and Degrees

Three Stages

1
Frostnip
Redness, numbness, skin firm but not frozen. Reversible with 30 min rewarming.
2
Superficial Frostbite
Outer layers frozen, blisters form after rewarming. Skin waxy/hard. Deep tissue still viable.
3
Deep Frostbite
All tissue layers frozen. Skin black and hard. Tissue death (gangrene) likely. Blood-filled blisters.

Four Degrees of Frostbite

DegreeTissue InvolvedAppearanceOutcome
1st Degree (Frostnip)Epidermis onlyRed, numb, slightly swollenFull recovery
2nd DegreeEpidermis + upper dermisBlisters with clear fluidUsually full recovery
3rd DegreeFull dermisDark/blood-filled blistersPartial tissue loss possible
4th DegreeExtends to tendons, bones, musclesDark, rubbery, mummifiedAmputation likely

Cold Protection Principles

  • Vapor-permeable materials: Allow moisture (sweat) to escape while blocking wind/cold. Avoid vapor-impermeable materials that trap sweat (creates wet clothing → conducts heat away 25× faster than dry air)
  • Mittens vs gloves: Below 0°C, mittens are preferred (fingers share heat); gloves are inadequate for extreme cold
  • Layering principle: Base layer (moisture wicking) + insulating layer (traps air) + outer shell (wind/water barrier)
  • Keep extremities covered: Ears, fingers, nose, toes most vulnerable

Factors Affecting Work Environment Design ★★★ Exam

The ergonomic objective of work environment design is to control environmental factors to maintain worker health, comfort, and productivity. The key factors are:

  1. Temperature (Thermal Environment): Air temperature, radiant heat from hot surfaces, metabolic heat load. Control measures: HVAC, shielding hot surfaces, local cooling, scheduling heat-intensive work during cooler periods.
  2. Humidity: Affects evaporative cooling capacity and thermal comfort. Optimal 40–50% RH. Control: dehumidification (summer), humidification (winter).
  3. Air Movement / Ventilation: Provides cooling and removes contaminants. Minimum 0.1 m/s to prevent stuffiness; maximum to avoid cold drafts.
  4. Illumination / Lighting: Adequate luminance for visual tasks; avoidance of glare and flicker. (Covered in Chapter 4.)
  5. Noise: Affects concentration, communication, hearing. Control through engineering and administrative measures. (Covered in Chapter 5.)
  6. Radiation: UV (skin damage, eye damage), infrared (heat absorption), ionizing radiation (DNA damage). Shielding, PPE, exposure limits.
  7. Chemical Hazards / Air Quality: Dust, fumes, vapors. Local exhaust ventilation, dilution ventilation.
  8. Vibration: Whole-body and hand-arm vibration. Anti-vibration mountings, vibration-damping materials.
PYQ (2018-19): Which objective of studying ergonomics is accomplished by work environment design and how? → Answer: The objective of fitting the work to the worker. By controlling environmental stressors (temperature, lighting, noise), the work environment is optimized so human physiological and psychological capacities are not overwhelmed, enabling safe and productive work.

2022-23 · Jan 2021 · 2015-16★★★ 3× · Q3b/Q7a/Q7a · 5–15 marks
Explain different health issues arising from unhealthy exposure to high-temperature work environments. / What is heat hyperpyrexia? Discuss its basic first aid procedures. / Discuss symptoms and basic first aid procedures for different heat illnesses.
Heat Illnesses — Spectrum from mild to severe:

1. Heat Cramps
Cause: Excessive sweating → loss of electrolytes (Na, K) → muscle cramping.
Symptoms: Painful muscle spasms (usually calves, abdomen).
First Aid: Move to cool area; rehydrate with electrolyte drink; gentle stretching. NOT water alone (dilutes electrolytes further).

2. Heat Syncope
Cause: Peripheral vasodilation (blood pools in skin vessels to cool the body) + dehydration → reduced cerebral blood flow → fainting.
Symptoms: Lightheadedness, brief loss of consciousness, pale cool skin.
First Aid: Lay down with feet elevated; cool environment; rehydrate.

3. Heat Exhaustion
Cause: Significant dehydration + electrolyte loss; blood flow inadequate to both muscles and skin cooling.
Symptoms: Heavy sweating, weakness, cold/pale/moist skin, weak pulse, nausea, possible fainting. Core temperature < 40°C.
First Aid: Move to cool area, lay down, loosen clothing, apply cool wet cloths, rehydrate. If vomiting, IV fluids needed → hospital.

4. Heat Stroke (Heat Hyperpyrexia)
Cause: Thermoregulatory system overwhelmed; core temperature ≥ 40°C (104°F). MEDICAL EMERGENCY.
Symptoms: HIGH body temperature (≥40°C), hot DRY skin (sweating ceases — classic heat stroke), rapid strong pulse, confusion/unconsciousness, possible seizures.
First Aid: Call emergency services immediately; rapid cooling (ice packs to neck/armpits/groin; cold water immersion if available); do NOT give fluids to unconscious person; fan while misting with cool water.
2022-23 · 2020-21★ 2× · Q2c/Q8b · 5/15 marks
(c) What is the significance of heat acclimatization on a production floor? / (b) What is heat acclimatization? Briefly describe different types of heat illness.
Heat Acclimatization: The physiological adaptation process that occurs when the body is repeatedly exposed to heat stress over 7–14 days. It is a physiological (not psychological) adaptation.

Physiological changes during acclimatization:
• Plasma volume expansion: Blood volume increases ~10–25% → more fluid available for sweating and circulation.
• Earlier onset of sweating: Sweating begins at a lower core temperature threshold → more efficient early cooling.
• Increased sweat rate: Can increase from ~1 L/hr to 2–3 L/hr in acclimatized individuals.
• Sweat becomes more dilute: Lower NaCl concentration in sweat → less electrolyte loss per litre.
• Lower core temperature and heart rate for the same work in the same heat.
• Improved cardiovascular efficiency: Less strain on the heart.

Significance on a production floor:
• Reduces heat illness risk dramatically (acclimatized workers can tolerate conditions that would cause heat exhaustion in non-acclimatized workers).
• OSHA recommends acclimatization protocol: start new workers at 20% heat exposure, increase by 20% each day over 5 days (or 7–14 days for severe heat).
• Improves productivity and reduces absenteeism in hot environments.
• Reduces workers' compensation claims for heat-related illness.
2018-19Q8c · 15 marks
(c) Which objective of studying ergonomics is accomplished by work environment design and how? Describe in detail the factors affecting work environment design.
Ergonomic objective accomplished: Work environment design primarily addresses health and safety (reducing physiological stress, illness, and injury from environmental factors) and secondarily productivity and comfort (maintaining cognitive and physical performance in adverse conditions).

Factors affecting work environment design:

1. Thermal Environment:
• Temperature (dry bulb), humidity (relative humidity), air velocity (wind chill / convection cooling), radiant heat (WBGT — Wet Bulb Globe Temperature index used for comprehensive assessment).
• Design: Ventilation, air conditioning, radiant heat shields, insulation, appropriate PPE (cooling vests).

2. Lighting:
• Illuminance level (lux), glare control, colour rendering index, uniformity ratio.
• Design: Task-appropriate lux levels (500 lux office, 100 lux corridors), anti-glare diffusers, daylight integration.

3. Noise:
• Sound pressure level (dB), frequency spectrum, duration of exposure, background vs. impulsive noise.
• Design: Engineering controls (enclosures, damping), administrative controls (job rotation), PPE (earmuffs).

4. Air Quality:
• Chemical contaminants (dust, fumes, VOCs), biological agents, CO₂ levels.
• Design: Ventilation, filtration, extraction at source.

5. Vibration:
• Whole-body vibration (vehicles) and hand-arm vibration (tools).
• Design: Anti-vibration mounts, vibration-damping handles, work-rest schedules.
