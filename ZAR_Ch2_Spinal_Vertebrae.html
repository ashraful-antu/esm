Chapter 2: Spinal Vertebrae & Discs

IPE 407 — Ergonomics & Safety Management  |  ZAR (Rafi Madam)  |  Exam Importance: ★★★★★

Overview

This chapter covers the anatomy of the spine, the structure of intervertebral discs, common disc pathologies, and the relationship between posture and disc health. Understanding spinal biomechanics is essential for designing workstations and equipment that protect workers from back disorders — the leading cause of occupational disability.

The Vertebral Column

The vertebral (spinal) column is the central structural element of the axial skeleton. It provides structural support, protects the spinal cord, and enables movement in multiple planes.

Natural Spinal Curves

When viewed from the side, the spine has four natural curves:

  • Cervical lordosis: Inward curve (concave posterior) — normal
  • Thoracic kyphosis: Outward curve (convex posterior) — normal
  • Lumbar lordosis: Inward curve (concave posterior) — normal ★ KEY
  • Sacral kyphosis: Outward curve — fixed by fusion

These curves distribute load and absorb shock. Deviation from these curves (e.g., lumbar kyphosis from slumped sitting) increases disc loading and injury risk.

Lumbar Vertebra Anatomy

The lumbar vertebrae (L1–L5) are the largest, bearing the most compressive load:

  • Vertebral body: Large, cylindrical — primary load-bearing element
  • Vertebral arch (neural arch): Encloses the spinal cord
  • Pedicle: Short pillar connecting the arch to the body (×2)
  • Lamina: Flat plate forming the posterior arch (×2)
  • Spinous process: Posterior projection (felt through skin as the "bumps" on the spine)
  • Transverse process: Lateral projections (×2) — muscle attachment points
  • Superior/Inferior articular processes: Form facet joints with adjacent vertebrae
  • Intervertebral foramen: Opening between pedicles for spinal nerve exit

Intervertebral Disc Structure ★★★★★ Exam (3×)

Intervertebral discs sit between adjacent vertebral bodies (C2–S1 = 23 discs). They provide:

  • Shock absorption under compressive load
  • Flexibility — allow spinal flexion, extension, rotation
  • Spacing — maintain proper foraminal height for nerve exits

Annulus Fibrosus

The outer ring of the disc, composed of 15–25 layers of concentric fibrocartilaginous rings (lamellae). Fibers in adjacent layers run in opposite directions (~30°), providing strength in multiple directions. The annulus confines the nucleus pulposus under load.

Function: Contains the nucleus; resists torsion, shear, and compression forces.

Nucleus Pulposus

The inner gelatinous core (~80% water in young adults). Has very high osmotic pressure, drawing water in and providing hydraulic cushioning. Under compressive load, it redistributes pressure radially to the annulus fibers.

Function: Hydraulic shock absorber; distributes compressive load evenly.

Disc Nutrition

Intervertebral discs are avascular (no blood supply after adolescence). They receive nutrients via diffusion from vertebral endplates during cyclic loading/unloading (walking, changing posture). Prolonged static loading prevents nutrient diffusion → accelerates disc degeneration.

This is why prolonged sitting is so harmful — static compression halts the pumping mechanism that nourishes the disc.

Types of Spinal Disc Conditions ★★★★★ Exam (3×)

Most Examined in Ch2 (3×): With neat sketches, differentiate between different types of spinal disc conditions.
1. Normal
Annulus intact; nucleus fully contained and hydrated; full disc height; no nerve impingement
2. Degenerative
Nucleus loses water content; disc height reduces; annulus may crack/tear internally; reduced shock absorption
3. Bulging
Annulus remains intact but weakens; nucleus pushes outward, creating a uniform bulge; may press on nerve roots
4. Herniated
Nucleus pulposus breaks through the annulus; can severely compress spinal nerves causing sciatica, radiating pain
5. Thinning
Advanced degeneration; disc height severely reduced; bone-on-bone contact possible; osteophytes may form

Detailed Description of Each Condition

Bulging Disc

The annulus fibrosus is weakened but intact. The nucleus pulposus pushes outward, creating a symmetrical bulge that extends beyond the normal disc perimeter. The nucleus has not broken through.

  • Cause: Cumulative repetitive loading, age-related annular weakening
  • Symptoms: May be asymptomatic; can cause local back pain if pressing on adjacent structures
  • Ergonomic relevance: Avoid sustained forward bending loads; encourage neutral spine postures

Herniated Disc (Disc Herniation / Slipped Disc)

The nucleus pulposus ruptures through a tear in the annulus fibrosus. The extruded material can compress adjacent nerve roots, causing severe radiating pain (sciatica if L4–L5 or L5–S1 herniates).

  • Cause: Acute high load in flexed/rotated spine position, or cumulative weakening
  • Symptoms: Severe localized pain; radiating pain/numbness/weakness in the leg (sciatica)
  • Treatment: Conservative (rest, physio, NSAIDs) → interventional (epidural) → surgical (discectomy)

Degenerative Disc Disease

Progressive loss of water content in the nucleus pulposus (from ~80% in young adult to ~70% by old age). Disc height decreases. Load-bearing transfers from nucleus to annulus → annular tears. Bone spurs (osteophytes) may form.

  • Risk factors: Age, obesity, repetitive loading, poor posture, smoking (reduces nutrient supply)
  • Ergonomic control: Encourage dynamic loading (movement, walking) rather than sustained static compression

Spondylolisthesis

Forward slip of one vertebra over the one below it. Can result from stress fracture of the pars interarticularis or degenerative facet joint disease. Causes spinal canal narrowing.

Disc Degeneration & Core Muscle Protection

Spinal discs are avascular — they depend on cyclic loading/unloading (the pumping action of movement) for nutrient supply. Core muscle strengthening protects against disc degeneration in several ways:

  • Load distribution: Strong core muscles share compressive loads with the disc, reducing disc pressure.
  • Spinal stability: Provide active stabilization of the spine, preventing shear forces that damage the annulus.
  • Posture maintenance: Maintain lumbar lordosis, preventing the disc loading asymmetries of flexed postures.
  • Movement promotion: Active core promotes dynamic rather than static loading patterns.
How strengthening core muscle may save you from spinal disk degeneration (biomechanical view): Core muscles act as a hydraulic corset (the multifidus and transverse abdominis particularly). When activated, they increase intra-abdominal pressure and create a pressurized cylinder that offloads the disc by up to 40%. They also stiffen the spine, reducing intersegmental shear. Without adequate core, all compressive and shear load falls on the passive disc.

Sitting Posture & Intervertebral Disc Pressure ★★★★★ Exam (3×)

Most Examined (3×): Discuss the relationship between sitting posture and intervertebral disc pressure.

Nachemson and Andersson's classic studies measured intradiscal pressure at L3 in various postures. Key findings:

PostureRelative Disc Pressure
Lying supine~25% (of standing baseline)
Relaxed standing100% (baseline)
Erect sitting (no support)~140% (+40% above standing)
Relaxed sitting (slight forward lean)~150%
Forward slumped sitting~190% (+90%) ← MAXIMUM
Sitting, backrest 90°~140% (+40%)
Sitting, backrest 100°~115% (+15%)
Sitting, backrest 110°~105% (+5%) ← OPTIMAL
Sitting, backrest 80° (leaned forward)~190% (+90%)

Why Sitting Increases Disc Pressure

When transitioning from standing to sitting:

  1. The pelvis rotates posteriorly (backward tilt)
  2. This flattens the lumbar lordosis → lumbar kyphosis
  3. The nucleus pulposus migrates posteriorly
  4. Posterior annular fibers are stretched and loaded asymmetrically
  5. Total disc pressure increases by ~40% above standing

Backrest Angle Effect

Increasing the backrest recline angle from 90° to 110° progressively shifts load from the disc to the backrest. At 110°, the disc pressure is only +5% above standing — the backrest bears most of the torso weight. Beyond 110° becomes impractical for work tasks.

Neck / Cervical Spine Issues

Neck Curve and Vertigo

Stiff neck and vertigo: The cervical spine contains proprioceptive receptors that contribute to balance and spatial orientation. When the neck muscles are chronically tense (from forward head posture — common with VDT use), proprioceptive signals become distorted → symptoms include dizziness, vertigo.

  • Sleeping posture that aggravates stiff neck: lying face-down with head turned to one side (maximum cervical rotation and compression)
  • Poor neck curve (cervical kyphosis or straightened cervical lordosis) develops from: prolonged VDT use, reading with head bowed, driving

Four To-Dos for Stiff Neck

  1. Cervical retraction exercises (chin tucks) — restore natural lordosis
  2. Monitor height adjustment — screen at or below eye level to reduce forward head posture
  3. Regular stretching of neck extensors and trapezius
  4. Ergonomic pillow (cervical support pillow) for sleep

Pelvis and Pelvic Tilt

The pelvis is the base of the spinal column. Its tilt directly affects lumbar curvature:

Pelvic Tilt TypeEffect on Lumbar SpineCommon Cause
Anterior tilt (hip flexors tight)Increases lumbar lordosis (arched back)Prolonged sitting, weak glutes

Anterior Pelvic Tilt (Scenario — Rafiq case, 2023-24)

Symptoms: overly curved lower back (hyperlordosis), abdomen protrudes, weak glute activation, tight hip flexors, lower back pain after sitting 8–10 hrs/day.

  • Problem: Anterior pelvic tilt syndrome (lower crossed syndrome)
  • Tight/overactive: Hip flexors (psoas, rectus femoris), lumbar erectors
  • Weak/underactive: Gluteus maximus, abdominal muscles, hamstrings
  • Corrective plan: Stretch hip flexors; strengthen glutes, hamstrings, deep abdominals; correct workstation height; scheduled breaks

Weight Gain from Poor Posture

Poor posture (slouched sitting) can contribute to weight gain indirectly: (1) Compressed abdominal organs reduce digestive efficiency; (2) Reduced metabolic rate from inactivity in poor posture; (3) Discomfort from poor posture discourages physical activity.

Chapter Summary

Key Takeaways

  1. Vertebral column: C7 + T12 + L5 + S5 + Co4 = 33 vertebrae; Lumbar = most load-bearing
  2. Disc structure: Annulus fibrosus (outer ring) + Nucleus pulposus (inner gel, 80% water)
  3. Disc conditions: Normal → Degenerative (thins) → Bulging (intact annulus) → Herniated (nucleus escapes) → Thinning (bone-on-bone)
  4. Disc nutrition: Avascular; depends on diffusion during cyclic loading — static postures starve the disc
  5. Disc pressure: Standing=100%; Erect sitting=+40%; Forward slump=+90%; Backrest 110°=only +5%
  6. Core muscles: Protect disc by offloading up to 40%, maintaining lumbar lordosis, reducing shear
  7. Neck: Forward head posture disturbs cervical proprioception → vertigo; lying face-down worst sleeping posture
  8. Pelvic tilt: Anterior = hyperlordosis (hip flexors tight, glutes weak); Posterior = kyphosis (slumped sitting)

2023-24Q4a · 15 marks
(a) Rafiq is a 24-year-old software engineering student. He works part-time as a remote web developer, sitting 8–10 hours/day without regular breaks. He has persistent lower back ache and hip tightness. When standing, his lower back appears overly curved and his abdomen protrudes. During workouts, his lower back lifts off the floor during leg raises; he struggles to engage his core during planks. A therapist notes a pelvic tilt during static posture assessment and observes poor glute activation. (i) What problem is Rafiq likely suffering from? (ii) Which muscles are tight/overactive and weak/underactive? (iii) Suggest a corrective plan.
(i) Problem: Anterior Pelvic Tilt (APT) — the front of the pelvis drops and the back rises, increasing lumbar lordosis. Classic presentation: protruding abdomen, exaggerated lumbar curve, lower back lifts during hip extension exercises.

(ii) Muscle imbalance pattern (Lower Crossed Syndrome — Janda):
Tight/Overactive: Hip flexors (iliopsoas, rectus femoris), lumbar erector spinae, TFL
Weak/Underactive: Gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, deep abdominals (transverse abdominis), hamstrings

(iii) Corrective plan:
• Ergonomic: Adjustable lumbar-support chair, forward-tilt seat pan, standing desk periods, break every 45 min.
• Stretching: Hip flexor stretches (kneeling lunge), quadriceps stretch, lumbar erector stretch (child's pose).
• Strengthening: Glute bridges, clamshells, dead bugs (transverse abdominis), bird-dogs, Romanian deadlifts.
• Movement: Walking breaks, avoid >90 min continuous sitting.

Discuss the relationship between sitting posture and intervertebral disc pressure.
Nachemson & Andersson's intradiscal pressure measurements (reference = standing = 100%):

• Standing: 100% (baseline)
• Walking: 115%
• Standing bent forward 20°: 150%
• Erect sitting (no backrest): 140% — posterior pelvic tilt flattens lordosis
• Relaxed sitting (lumbar kyphosis): 185–190% — worst posture for discs
• Sitting with lumbar support: 100–110% — lumbar support restores lordosis
• Reclined sitting (120°): 60–70% — trunk weight transferred to backrest
• Lying supine: 25%

Mechanism: Posterior pelvic tilt during sitting → lumbar kyphosis → posterior disc height decreases → nucleus pulposus migrates posteriorly → posterior annulus under tension → herniation risk. Maintained lordosis redistributes load to vertebral bodies and facets.

Design implication: Lumbar backrest support is essential. Reclined backrest (100–115°) + lumbar support = best combination for prolonged sitting.
2022-23 · 2021-22 · Jan 2021★★★ 3× · Q6c/Q5b/Q8b · 12–15 marks
With neat sketches, differentiate between different types of spinal disc conditions. / State the functions of a spinal disc. Contrast the different types of spinal disc conditions.
Functions of intervertebral disc: Shock absorption, flexibility (flexion/extension/rotation), and maintaining vertebral spacing for nerve exit.

Types of disc conditions (pathological progression):

1. Normal Disc: Nucleus pulposus (gel core) centered; annulus fibrosus (fibrous rings) intact; disc height maintained; no nerve contact.

2. Disc Bulge: Annulus weakens asymmetrically; disc margin extends beyond vertebral endplate edge but outer annulus is intact. Broad-based, symmetric. May cause mild nerve pressure.

3. Disc Protrusion (Herniation): Nucleus material pushes into outer annulus fibers; focal outward displacement. Outer annulus intact. Can press on nerve roots → radiculopathy (shooting pain down leg = sciatica).

4. Disc Extrusion: Nucleus pulposus breaks through the outer annulus; material still connected to disc. Severe nerve compression possible.

5. Disc Sequestration: Fragment of nucleus pulposus completely separates and migrates into spinal canal. Can compress spinal cord or cauda equina → emergency.

6. Disc Degeneration: Loss of water content (disc desiccation); disc height decreases; osteophytes form; reduced flexibility. Chronic, age-related. Treated conservatively.
Jan 2021Q6c · 15 marks
(c) As an ergonomic professional at an IKEA furniture retail company, you have analyzed complaint records and identified them associated with poor seat design and inappropriate sitting posture. You are presenting principles of seat design to alleviate complaint issues. Discuss the salient points of your presentation.
Presentation: Ergonomic Seat Design Principles to Reduce Spinal Complaints

1. Lumbar Support: Convex pad at L3–L4 to maintain lumbar lordosis; prevents lumbar kyphosis and reduces disc pressure from 185% to ~110% of standing baseline.

2. Seat Height Adjustability: Range 38–52 cm; feet flat on floor; hip and knee angles ≥ 90°; prevents posterior pelvic tilt from dangling feet.

3. Seat Depth (Sliding Pan): 3-finger clearance behind knees; avoids popliteal compression; reduces lower limb circulatory problems.

4. Backrest Recline (100–115°): Transfers trunk weight to backrest; reduces disc pressure significantly; reduces paraspinal muscle load.

5. Reduce Postural Fixity: Dynamic/floating seat mechanisms; encourage position changes; break every 30–45 min.

6. Armrests: At elbow height; reduce shoulder load; transfer some weight off spine (~10–15% load reduction).

7. Cushioning: Medium-firm; breathable; avoids both hard-edge pressure and "sinking-in" softness.

8. Consumer education: Correct sitting posture instruction cards; chair setup guide; reminder of movement breaks.
Jan 2021Q8a · 8 marks
(a) Do you think men are more prone to chronic pain issues compared to women? Explain your answer.
No — women are generally more prone to chronic pain conditions than men.

Evidence: Fibromyalgia (9:1 female:male), rheumatoid arthritis (3:1 female), migraines (3:1 female), irritable bowel syndrome (2:1 female), temporomandibular disorders (2:1 female).

Reasons women experience more chronic pain:
• Hormonal factors: Estrogen modulates pain sensitivity; fluctuating estrogen levels (menstrual cycle, menopause) affect pain thresholds. Progesterone has some protective effect.
• Neurological differences: Women have denser distribution of nociceptors (pain receptors) and more sensitive central sensitization pathways.
• Psychological/psychosocial: Higher rates of anxiety and depression (pain amplifiers) in women.
• Musculoskeletal differences: Lower muscle mass relative to body weight → more joint loading per unit muscle force; wider Q-angle (hip-to-knee) → more knee stress.
• Societal factors: Women historically underdiagnosed for pain — may report more because previously undertreated.

Exception — occupational injuries: Men have higher rates of acute workplace injuries (heavy industry) but women have higher rates of repetitive strain and chronic pain in sedentary jobs.
Jan 2021Q8c · 15 marks
(c) A factory worker carries load predominantly on the right side of his body. He experiences intense back pain, and during standing, he cannot lift his toes without pushing his hips backward. The plant physician prescribed painkillers. Was the diagnosis correct? What is the cause? Suggest solutions.
Diagnosis assessment: Prescribing only painkillers is incorrect and inadequate. Painkillers mask symptoms but do not address the biomechanical cause. The correct approach requires ergonomic and physiotherapy intervention.

Cause of symptoms:
• Asymmetric loading (right side): Creates lateral pelvic tilt → spinal scoliosis under load → asymmetric disc compression → disc pathology on loaded side.
• Inability to lift toes (anterior tibialis weakness) + hips pushing back: Classic sign of tight hip flexors / tight lumbar extensors combined with weak anterior chain (hip extensors, anterior tibialis). This suggests the spine is in excessive lordosis that is compensated by hip posterior shift — consistent with spondylolisthesis or severe anterior pelvic tilt.

Solutions:
• Ergonomic: Use material handling equipment (carts, hoists); balanced load carrying (both hands/sides); job rotation.
• Medical: Proper diagnosis (imaging for disc/nerve involvement); physiotherapy; core strengthening.
• Footwear: Assess whether footwear is contributing to altered gait.
• Postural training: Correct asymmetric loading habits.

2018-19Q3c · 12 marks
(c) Can stiff neck cause 'Vertigo'? How? What sleeping postures aggravate stiff neck? Explain with schematics. Mention four to-dos to get rid of stiff neck.
Can stiff neck cause vertigo? Yes.
Mechanism: Cervicogenic vertigo — tight muscles/restricted joints in the upper cervical spine (C1–C3) interfere with proprioceptive signals from neck mechanoreceptors to the vestibular system (balance center). The brain receives conflicting signals → dizziness/vertigo. Also: tight suboccipital muscles can compress the vertebral arteries → reduced blood flow to cerebellum → true vertigo.

Sleeping postures that aggravate stiff neck:
• Sleeping on stomach (prone): Forces head rotation 90° → sustained cervical rotation → muscle and joint strain.
• Sleeping on side with pillow too high or too low: Lateral flexion of cervical spine throughout the night.
• Sleeping with multiple pillows causing neck flexion.
• Using excessively firm/soft mattress without adequate neck support.

Four to-dos to get rid of stiff neck:
1. Apply heat (warm compress 15–20 min) to relax muscle spasm.
2. Gentle range-of-motion exercises (slow neck tilts, rotations — NOT forceful).
3. Ergonomic assessment: monitor height, pillow height correction.
4. Physiotherapy: soft tissue massage, mobilization, posture retraining.
2018-19Q3d · 6 marks
(d) Ms. X was advised to cut her diet for weight loss. She claimed she rarely overeats. The doctor told her to eat the same calories in 5–6 small meals instead of 2–3. This worked without cutting favorite foods. How does the same caloric intake spread over more meals burn more weight?
Mechanism: Thermic Effect of Food (TEF) and Metabolic Rate

Every time you eat, your body expends energy to digest, absorb, and metabolize the food — this is the Thermic Effect of Food (TEF), approximately 10% of total caloric intake.

Why more meals = more total TEF: Eating 6 small meals triggers 6 digestive responses; each meal "revs up" metabolism briefly. Though total TEF is similar to 2–3 large meals (TEF is proportional to calories consumed, not meal frequency), the key mechanisms are:

1. Insulin management: Large infrequent meals → large insulin spikes → more fat storage. Small frequent meals → lower, stable insulin → less fat storage signal.
2. Avoidance of fasting-induced fat storage: Long gaps between meals → body conserves energy → lower metabolic rate. Frequent meals prevent this adaptation.
3. Muscle protein synthesis: More frequent protein intake → better muscle maintenance → higher resting metabolic rate (muscle burns more calories than fat at rest).
4. Reduced overeating: Prevents extreme hunger that leads to binge eating.
2017-18Q1b · 6 marks
(b) How can strengthening core muscles save you from spinal disk degeneration at an early age? Explain from a biomechanical point of view.
Biomechanical role of core muscles in disc protection:

The core muscles (transverse abdominis, multifidus, internal obliques, diaphragm, pelvic floor) form a muscular cylinder around the lumbar spine. When activated, they:

1. Increase intra-abdominal pressure (IAP): The pressurized abdominal cavity acts as a hydraulic support pillar, reducing compressive load on lumbar discs by 20–40%.
2. Provide intersegmental stability: Multifidus controls micro-movements between vertebrae; prevents the abnormal shear forces that damage annular fibers.
3. Maintain lumbar lordosis: Prevents the kyphotic posture that maximizes disc pressure.
4. Distribute load: Strong core transfers some spinal load to the pelvis and thoracic cage, reducing the share borne by lumbar discs.

Without strong core: Every spine movement involves uncontrolled micro-shear → progressive annular fiber damage → disc degeneration accelerated.

Evidence: Subjects with chronic low back pain show atrophy of multifidus; core training programs reduce recurrence of disc-related back pain.
2017-18 · 2016-17★ 2× · Q2b/Q3c · 8/9 marks
(b) How can you restore a bad neck curve? Explain with necessary schematics. / (c) What types of tasks deteriorate your neck curve? Briefly explain.
Tasks that deteriorate neck curve (cervical kyphosis / loss of lordosis):
• Prolonged forward head posture (computer use, smartphone use — "text neck")
• Jobs requiring downward gaze (sewing, microscopy, dental work)
• Driving with poorly positioned headrest
• Sleeping prone (forces neck rotation and loss of lordosis)
• Heavy lifting with forward head posture

How to restore bad neck curve:
1. Chin tucks (cervical retraction): Pull chin straight back (not down) — activates deep cervical flexors; counteracts forward head. Hold 5 sec, 10 reps × 3 sets daily.
2. Cervical extension exercises: Gently tilt head back over a rolled towel or cervical roll.
3. Ergonomic correction: Raise monitor to eye level; use document holder; adjust car headrest.

2022-23Q7b · 10 marks
(b) How is pelvic tilt related to pregnancy? What would be your suggestions for fixing pelvic tilt after the pregnancy period?
Pelvic tilt and pregnancy:
During pregnancy, the growing uterus shifts the center of gravity forward → body compensates with increased lumbar lordosis and anterior pelvic tilt. The added weight of the fetus/uterus (~3–5 kg at term) also stretches abdominal muscles (rectus abdominis — diastasis recti possible) and places more load on lumbar discs and sacroiliac joints.

Relaxin hormone released during pregnancy loosens ligaments (preparation for birth) → hypermobility of pelvic joints → pelvic girdle pain, symphysis pubis dysfunction.

Fixing post-pregnancy pelvic tilt:
• Wait for relaxin levels to normalize (6–12 weeks postpartum) before intensive exercise.
• Core rehabilitation: Start with diaphragmatic breathing → transverse abdominis activation → progress to planks, bird-dogs.
• Gluteal strengthening: Bridges, clamshells, squats — to counteract anterior tilt.
• Hip flexor stretching: Kneeling lunge stretch (shortened during pregnancy).
• Postural awareness: Avoid prolonged standing in exaggerated lordosis; use supportive footwear.
• Pelvic floor therapy: With a women's health physiotherapist for comprehensive rehabilitation.
Jan 2021Q4a · 10 marks
(a) Your team is tasked with designing a motorbike saddle for women only. You decide to design the saddle for proper posture but sacrifice comfort completely. Suggest specifications for the saddle that will ensure proper posture during driving. Does this design violate ergonomic design principles? Explain.
Saddle specifications for proper posture (women-specific):
• Width: Match female ischial tuberosity spacing (wider than male average — women's sit bones are ~10–15% wider). Approximately 15–17 cm seat width at tuberosity contact zone.
• Nose width: Narrow to prevent perineal pressure and allow leg freedom.
• Tilt: Level or 2–3° nose-down to prevent forward sliding and reduce perineal pressure.
• Firmness: Firm base (no excessive foam sinking) for pelvic stability; bony prominences receive firm support — maintains anterior pelvic tilt and lumbar lordosis.
• Height: Adjusted so both feet can touch the ground with slight bend in knees (safety) OR for sport riding: pedal at bottom position with ~20–25° knee bend.

Does this violate ergonomic principles?
Yes, partially. Ergonomics encompasses both performance (posture) AND well-being (comfort, injury prevention). Sacrificing comfort entirely violates the holistic definition. A properly designed ergonomic seat should achieve both. However, in motorcycle design, posture for safety and control is prioritized — the compromise is acceptable if discomfort is minimal and not injurious.
Jan 2021Q4b · 12 marks
(b) State some design considerations for the saddle (from above scenario) that ensure the pelvis does not experience undue strain. In your opinion, can men also use these bikes? Explain.
Design considerations to prevent pelvic strain:
• Adequate width for ischial tuberosities: Support bony prominences, not soft tissue. Saddle width must match sit-bone spacing to prevent soft-tissue compression.
• Central cutout or pressure relief channel: Reduces perineal (soft tissue) pressure — important for both sexes but especially for women (labia/perineal anatomy).
• Lumbar support or backrest: For extended riding, a backrest maintains lumbar lordosis and reduces pelvic rocking.
• Pelvic stabilization: Saddle shape should prevent lateral pelvic tilt during riding (caused by asymmetric weight shifts).
• Footpeg position: Height and forward position of footpegs affects hip angle → affects pelvic tilt.
• Vibration damping: Anti-vibration seat material to protect sacroiliac joints and discs from road vibration.

Can men use these bikes?
Not optimally. Male ischial tuberosity spacing is ~10–15% narrower than female. A saddle designed for female sit-bone spacing will be too wide for men → weight falls between tuberosities onto soft tissue → perineal pressure → numbness, impaired circulation. The posture-focused features (lumbar support, firmness, cutout) would still benefit men, but saddle width needs to be gender-specific.

(i) Type of pelvic tilt in forward-slumped seated computer posture:

(ii) Muscles involved in posterior pelvic tilt:
Tight/shortened: Hamstrings (biceps femoris, semimembranosus, semitendinosus) — pull the ischium down → rotate pelvis posteriorly. Rectus abdominis (shortened in slumped posture).
Lengthened/weak: Hip flexors (iliopsoas), lumbar erector spinae.

How they affect back and neck:
• Posterior pelvic tilt → lumbar kyphosis → increased posterior disc pressure (185% of standing) → disc herniation risk.
• Forward slumped lumbar spine causes compensatory thoracic hyperkyphosis → shoulders round → head protrudes forward (forward head posture).
• Forward head (+1 cm = +2–3 kg load on cervical spine) → chronic neck muscle tension, cervicogenic headaches, possible cervical disc compression.
• Rounded shoulders → shoulder impingement risk.
