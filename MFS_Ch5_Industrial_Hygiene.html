AREC Model — The Industrial Hygiene Framework

The AREC Framework

A — Anticipation
Predict potential hazards before they occur. Review process descriptions, Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDS/SDS), historical data, and planned changes to identify risks proactively.
R — Recognition
Identify existing or potential health hazards in the workplace through walk-through surveys, process analysis, and worker interviews. Categorize hazards as chemical, physical, biological, or ergonomic.
E — Evaluation
Measure and assess the magnitude of exposure using air sampling, biological monitoring, and noise dosimetry. Compare against occupational exposure limits (TLV, PEL, REL) to determine risk level.
C — Control
Implement measures to eliminate or reduce exposure below acceptable limits, following the hierarchy: Engineering → Administrative → PPE.

Routes of Entry

RouteDescriptionExamples
InhalationMost common and most dangerous route. Substances enter via the respiratory tract.Dust, vapors, gases, fumes from welding, spraying, chemical processes
Skin Contact / AbsorptionSubstances penetrate through the skin. Can be systemic (enters bloodstream) or local effect.Solvents, pesticides, corrosives — dermal absorption of organic solvents
IngestionSwallowing contaminated food/water or hand-to-mouth contactLead dust on hands, contaminated food in workplaces using chemicals
InjectionPiercing of skin by contaminated sharps or high-pressure fluid injectionNeedle-stick injuries in healthcare, grease gun injection injuries

Toxicology & Dose-Response

Key Toxicology Concepts

"The dose makes the poison" (Paracelsus, ~1500) — Every substance can be harmful at a sufficient dose. The dose-response relationship describes how the effect changes as dose increases.

Classification of Toxic Effects

ClassificationDefinitionExample
AcuteEffects occurring rapidly after a single or short-term high exposureCO poisoning from leaking heater — immediate headache, dizziness, death
ChronicEffects occurring after repeated long-term low-level exposure over months/yearsSilicosis from years of silica dust exposure; lead poisoning in battery workers
LocalEffect occurs at the site of contact — does not enter bloodstreamAcid burn on skin; eye irritation from chemical splash
SystemicSubstance enters bloodstream and affects organs/systems distant from entry siteMercury vapor inhaled → affects kidneys and nervous system; lead → affects brain

Types of Chemical Interactions

  • Additive — combined effect equals sum of individual effects (1+1=2). E.g., two solvents with similar mechanisms
  • Synergistic — combined effect is greater than sum (1+1=4). E.g., alcohol + acetaminophen liver toxicity (classic exam question!)
  • Antagonistic — one substance reduces the effect of another (1+1=0.5). E.g., antidote blocking a poison
  • Potentiation — a non-toxic substance enhances toxicity of another (0+1=4)

Exam tip: Alcohol + acetaminophen (Tylenol) = Synergistic interaction. Both are individually processed by the liver; together they cause much greater liver damage than either alone. This appears as a 2014-15 question.

Occupational Exposure Limits

Three Major OEL Standards

StandardAgencyLegal StatusNotes
TLV (Threshold Limit Value)ACGIH (American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists)Voluntary guidelines, not lawUpdated annually; most widely used by IH professionals; more current than PELs
PEL (Permissible Exposure Limit)OSHALegally enforceableBased on 1968 TLVs; many outdated; citation issued if exceeded
REL (Recommended Exposure Limit)NIOSHRecommendations, not lawOften more protective than PELs; intended to prevent occupational diseases

Three Types of TLV

TLV-TWA
Time-Weighted Average — the average concentration for a normal 8-hour workday / 40-hour work week. The most common OEL.
TLV-STEL
Short-Term Exposure Limit — maximum concentration for 15-minute exposure. Cannot exceed 4 times per day, with ≥60 min between exposures.
TLV-C
Ceiling — concentration that should NEVER be exceeded even instantaneously. Used for substances with severe immediate effects (e.g., HCN).

OSHA vs. ACGIH Standards

  • OSHA PELs are legally enforceable but many are outdated (based on 1968 data)
  • ACGIH TLVs are more protective and updated annually but voluntary
  • Industrial hygienists typically use TLVs for practice; OSHA uses PELs for enforcement
  • NIOSH RELs often represent the lowest feasible exposure

Health Hazard Controls — Hierarchy

  1. Engineering Controls — Most effective. Examples: local exhaust ventilation, process enclosure, substitution of less toxic material, wet methods to suppress dust
  2. Administrative Controls — Change work practices. Examples: job rotation, limiting time in high-exposure areas, work practice controls, training
  3. PPE — Last resort. Examples: respirators, chemical-resistant gloves, hearing protection. Requires fit-testing, training, and ongoing maintenance program

Quick Review

What does AREC stand for?
Anticipation, Recognition, Evaluation, Control — the 4 steps of industrial hygiene practice
What are the 4 routes of chemical entry?
Inhalation (most common), Skin absorption, Ingestion, Injection
What is TLV-TWA vs TLV-STEL vs TLV-C?
TWA: 8-hr average. STEL: 15-min max (4x/day). C: Never exceed even instantaneously (ceiling).
What is the difference between PEL and TLV?
PEL (OSHA) = legally enforceable, based on 1968 data, often outdated. TLV (ACGIH) = voluntary, updated annually, more protective.
What type of interaction is alcohol + acetaminophen?
Synergistic — combined liver toxicity is much greater than the sum of individual effects (1+1 > 2)
What is "acute vs chronic" toxicity?
Acute: rapid effect from single/short-term high dose. Chronic: slow effect from repeated long-term low-level exposure.
Why is PPE the last resort?
PPE requires ongoing worker compliance, fit testing, and maintenance. Engineering controls work passively without depending on worker behavior.
What is the NIOSH REL?
Recommended Exposure Limit from NIOSH. Not legally enforceable but represents what NIOSH believes is achievable and health-protective.
